\begin{abstract}
A robot learning across its service life must decide what to keep in fixed-size
memory. The episodic buffer in e-MDB, a cognitive architecture for lifelong
robot learning, discards the oldest entry whatever its value. We present EMBER
(Emergent Memory-Based Encoding and Reactivation), which treats such a memory as
a six-axis search space: how a trace is read, written, weighted by surprise,
decayed, evicted, and reinforced. Their combinations give
\result{exp03_axis_liveness:n_genotipos|int}{576} \emph{configurations} of one
engine; we evaluate every one, including a nearest-neighbour proxy of the
e-MDB buffer. Each is
scored on how many important-marked experiences survive a stream overrunning the
memory many times over. The memory never sees the marks, only a surprise
signal. We report four results. First, which mechanism matters depends on $r = K_{proto}/C$, recurring
situations divided by capacity. Below $r=1$ the write axis explains
\result{exp02_threshold_grid:regimenes.compression.eta2_write|pct}{73.0}\,\% of
the variance in retention; above it,
\result{exp02_threshold_grid:regimenes.selection.eta2_write|pct}{1.6}\,\%, and
eviction takes over. The switch falls at the same $r$ for three capacities, which
we state as a hypothesis for the conditions tested, not a general law. Second, the best configuration retains
\result{exp01_nas_full:frontier.scores.rare_retention|f3}{0.983} of the marked
experiences against
\result{exp01_nas_full:incumbent.scores.rare_retention|f3}{0.050} for that
proxy, differing on two axes only: surprise-weighted encoding and
weakest-trace eviction. Neither alone changes anything. Third, a
purely spiking circuit fails the reconstruction test the others pass: it was
built for discrete patterns, not continuous vectors. Fourth, two preconditions
appear only on real data. Merging needs repeated encounters to look alike;
embeddings of natural images do not
(\result{exp05_real_embeddings:precondicion_cifar100.similitud_intra_prototipo|f3}{0.401}
against
\result{exp05_real_embeddings:precondicion_cifar100.umbral_de_fusion|f2}{0.85}).
Surprise must come from reward, not perceptual novelty, raising retention
from
\result{exp06_minigrid:comparacion_aleatoria_prediccion.resultados.frontera.tasa|pct}{0.0}\,\%
to
\result{exp06_minigrid:comparacion_aleatoria_recompensa.resultados.frontera.tasa|pct}{52.6}\,\%.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A cognitive robot that learns over its lifetime faces a memory problem that goes
well beyond mere storage. It must decide what is worth keeping, keep that
information accessible in proportion to its relevance, and retrieve it reliably
even from a partial or degraded cue. These three requirements map onto three
well-studied properties of biological memory: selective encoding modulated by
surprise and novelty, use-regulated trace consolidation and decay, and
content-addressable retrieval that is robust to noise.

The episodic buffer of the e-MDB (\emph{epistemic Multilevel Darwinist Brain})
architecture \cite{romero2022thesis,romero2023ica} ---in its public ROS2
implementation, a fixed-capacity FIFO (first-in-first-out) queue
\cite{emdbnodes}--- satisfies none of the three. It keeps whatever was
perceived most recently, treating every experience alike regardless of its
importance, novelty or prediction error. Episodes are read back by position or
as whole training batches, not by content. When the buffer fills, the oldest
episode is simply discarded: it is, in effect, a database with no criterion.

The bioinspired-memory literature offers several principled remedies. Kanerva's
Sparse Distributed Memory (SDM) \cite{kanerva1988} provides content-addressable
retrieval over a distributed superposition of memory locations, with graceful
degradation under noise. Hebbian engram consolidation \cite{liu2012,hebb1949}
offers a biologically plausible mechanism for reinforcing traces through
repeated co-activation. Prediction-error gating regulates how strongly a new
experience is encoded as a function of how surprising it is, grounded in
dopaminergic modulation of hippocampal encoding by novelty and motivational
significance \cite{lisman2005,shohamy2010} and in the finding that larger
reward-prediction errors improve episodic memory for the events that produced
them \cite{rouhani2018}. And forgetting need not be indiscriminate: memory
availability tracks how likely an item is to be needed again
\cite{anderson1991}, and transience is an actively regulated process that
serves decision-making rather than a failure of storage \cite{richards2017},
which motivates eviction by estimated value rather than by age when capacity is
exceeded.

But these mechanisms are almost always evaluated in isolation, embedded in
architectures purpose-built to show them off, and compared against baselines
that do not share their design space. That makes it hard ---sometimes
impossible--- to tell whether a reported gain comes from the mechanism itself or
from an incidental advantage of the evaluation protocol.

This work reports on EMBER (Emergent Memory-Based Encoding and Reactivation), an
investigation of long-term episodic memory for cognitive robots, and it
inverts the usual approach. Rather than proposing a new architecture and arguing
that it beats a simple baseline, we define a discrete design space
in which each bioinspired mechanism is an axis, and we traverse it
exhaustively. The e-MDB FIFO ---more precisely, a FIFO-NN proxy of it that adds
nearest-neighbour retrieval--- is not an external baseline but one more point
inside that space, which turns a potentially circular comparison into a
falsifiable question: where does the deployed \emph{incumbent} land, and which
combination of mechanisms defines the \emph{performance frontier}, the space's
best-scoring point?

We then evaluate the bioinspired architectures that emerge from that analysis
with a two-phase benchmark separating the ability to reconstruct a degraded cue
---a prerequisite of any associative memory--- from retaining the right episodes
under continuous capacity pressure. Finally, we instrument the regime hypothesis and the
salience mechanism on two real domains ---image embeddings from CIFAR-100
\cite{krizhevsky2009}, a 100-class benchmark of natural images, and the MiniGrid
\cite{minigrid} navigation environment under partial observability--- exposing
preconditions no synthetic design reveals, one of which we correct.

Three terms recur throughout. A \emph{surprise signal} is a scalar
attached to an experience when it is written: either \emph{novelty}, which the
memory computes as one minus the cosine similarity to the closest thing it
holds, or \emph{prediction error} in $[0,1]$, from a model of what was
expected. The \emph{salience
gate} turns that signal into an encoding weight. \emph{Rare-event retention} is
the fraction of importance-tagged episodes still recoverable after a stream
longer than the capacity (sixteen times longer in the main search); the tag is ground truth
used only for scoring, never visible to the memory, which is what keeps the
measurement from being circular and why eviction by age is worst placed under
it.

Two more terms recur from Section \ref{sec:nas} on. A \emph{configuration} is one
complete memory design ---a choice on each of the six axes; the
\result{exp03_axis_liveness:n_genotipos|int}{576} configurations are enumerated
exhaustively, not sampled or optimized. The ratio $r = K_{proto}/C$
relates $K_{proto}$, the number of \emph{prototypes} in the stream (distinct
recurring kinds of experience, formally the latent clusters its keys are drawn
from), to $C$, the \emph{capacity}: the fixed number of traces the memory holds
before a write forces an eviction. Thus $r<1$ means the recurring experience fits
with slots to spare, $r>1$ that it does not; $K_{proto}$ is known on synthetic
streams and estimated on real ones (Section \ref{sec:real}).

The main contributions of this work are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item A \emph{regime hypothesis}, supported under the conditions evaluated: the
  ratio $r = K_{proto}/C$ between recurring experience prototypes and memory
  capacity determines which family of mechanisms governs performance, with a
  regime transition that reproduces at three distinct capacities of one
  stationary synthetic generator.
  \item A demonstration that in a design space of gated mechanisms, main
  effects mislead. Modulating encoding strength by novelty and prediction
  error ---the most-cited mechanism in the bioinspired-memory literature---
  explains barely
  \result{exp01_nas_full:main_effects.3.eta2|pct}{1.4}\,\% of the variance as a
  main effect, yet conditioned on the one eviction policy that reads the
  strength signal it improves rare-event retention by a factor of
  \result{exp01_nas_full:salience_conditional_ratio|f2}{14.41}. The frontier
  architecture differs from the deployed incumbent on two axes only, and
  neither is worth installing without the other.
  \item A two-phase benchmark that admits architectures by reconstruction
  before exposing them to capacity pressure, revealing that a pure spiking
  circuit (leaky integrate-and-fire neurons, LIF, with spike-timing-dependent
  plasticity, STDP) does not transfer to the continuous-vector domain
  without an intermediate encoding layer.
  \item Two preconditions that appear only on real data. Consolidation
  requires a merge threshold that separates repeated encounters from distinct
  ones, which on CIFAR-100 none does: there is no threshold and no $r$,
  nominal or effective, at which a compression regime exists. And the salience mechanism requires surprise to
  be anchored in the variable that decides what matters ---reward--- rather
  than in the perceptual novelty of the state; unlike the first, this one we
  identify and correct.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}

EMBER instantiates four memory substrates, evaluated in Section
\ref{sec:benchmark}: Sparse Distributed Memory (SDM), the Engram Neural Network
(ENN, a construct of this work), a pure spiking circuit, and a hybrid
Spiking-SDM. Their roots follow, with those of the search method and the
deployment target.

\subsection{Sparse Distributed Memory}

Kanerva's Sparse Distributed Memory \cite{kanerva1988} models associative memory
on the high-dimensional structure of the nervous system. Storing information in
one location is fragile; instead patterns are distributed across many
overlapping \emph{hard locations} with random addresses, so a noisy query still
activates enough of the same ones to reconstruct the original. A write activates
the locations within a similarity radius of the queried address; a read sums and
thresholds those same locations.

Concretely, in the instantiation used here 512 hard locations are drawn once as
random unit vectors and never move. A write adds $s \cdot k$ to the counters of
the 25 locations ($5\,\%$) most aligned with the key $k$, where $s$ is the
encoding strength, so the episode is smeared across twenty-five locations and
stored in none; a read sums the counters of the query's 25 nearest locations and
normalizes. A noisy query selects a slightly different active set, but the
overlap is large enough to reconstruct the right pattern, so accuracy degrades
gradually rather than abruptly --- the property that matters for a robot, whose
sensors are never clean. Our instantiation departs from the textbook model in
two ways: activation is by inner product on a continuous address space rather
than a Hamming radius, so the radius self-adapts to local density
(\cite{kanerva2009} extends this to hyperdimensional computing); and it is a
hybrid, keeping an explicit trace list alongside the counters and returning the
stored trace most similar to the reconstruction, so exact payloads survive.

\subsection{Engrams, Hebbian consolidation and STDP}

The \emph{engram} ---the physical trace a memory leaves in neural tissue--- was
coined by Semon \cite{semon1904} and confirmed experimentally in modern
neuroscience \cite{liu2012}. Its cellular mechanism is long-term potentiation
(LTP): neurons that fire together repeatedly strengthen the synapse between
them, the principle Hebb \cite{hebb1949} distilled into his dictum. The
\emph{Engram Neural Network} (ENN) is the name we give in this work ---it is not
a named architecture from the prior literature--- to the minimal construction
that turns this principle into an online memory: a matrix of fast Hebbian
weights updated without backpropagation, where each write creates a trace or
reinforces an existing one above a cosine threshold, with initial strength
proportional to prediction error and exponential decay without reinforcement.

Spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP) \cite{bipoo1998} is the same rule at
the level of the individual spike: a synapse strengthens when the presynaptic
neuron fires just before the postsynaptic one, and weakens in the opposite
order. This groups neurons into assemblies, leaving a structural trace that is
precisely an engram. EMBER's PoC-3 ---the third of the project's
proof-of-concept notebooks--- verified this on leaky integrate-and-fire
neurons: after repeated presentation, intra-group weights rose well above
inter-group weights, and a partial cue recruited the rest of its group without
activating the others. Whether that mechanism extends to continuous input
vectors is what Section \ref{sec:benchmark} tests.

\subsection{Architecture search and prioritized replay}

Neural architecture search \cite{elsken2019} treats architectural decisions as
optimizable variables. Its application to the memory mechanism itself is far
less explored: Neural Turing Machines \cite{graves2014} and related
memory-augmented architectures \cite{munkhdalai2017} fix the memory type and
learn how to read and write it. We instead make the memory mechanism the
search variable, over a fully enumerable space, so that no result can be
attributed to an approximate optimizer.

The closest analogue outside neuroscience is deep reinforcement learning.
Prioritized experience replay \cite{schaul2016} weights which transition to
revisit by temporal-difference error ---the same ``surprising things count
more'' principle that motivates prediction-error gating--- and Neural Episodic
Control \cite{pritzel2017} indexes a differentiable dictionary by similarity,
analogous to SDM's content-addressable read. In both, the priority and read
mechanisms are chosen in advance. This work treats those choices as the search
space, and measures in which regime each one matters.

Memory selection under a fixed budget also has a long non-biological history.
Cache replacement evicts by recency or frequency of use (LRU, LFU and their
refinements) \cite{oneil1993,megiddo2003}; reservoir sampling keeps a uniform
sample of an unbounded stream in fixed space \cite{vitter1985}, and is a strong
baseline for replay buffers in continual learning \cite{chaudhry2019}. Rehearsal
methods choose which exemplars to keep by herding towards class means
\cite{rebuffi2017}, by gradient diversity \cite{aljundi2019}, or, closest to
this work, by surprise, reward, distribution matching or state-space coverage
for lifelong reinforcement learning \cite{isele2018}; replay itself mitigates forgetting in
that setting \cite{rolnick2019}. These methods optimize downstream learning with
a fixed selection rule; EMBER instead measures retention across a space of rules.
Our \texttt{fifo}, \texttt{min\_utility} (least-read, LFU-like) and
\texttt{random} eviction options are the simplest members of these families; the
design space does not contain reservoir, LRU or prioritized-sampling rules.

\subsection{The e-MDB architecture and the LOLA problem}

e-MDB, developed by the Integrated Group for Engineering Research at the
Universidade da Coru\~na, descends from the Multilevel Darwinist Brain
\cite{bellas2010} and targets the LOLA problem (\emph{Lifelong Open-ended
Learning Autonomy}) \cite{romero2023sensors}: a robot that acquires and relates
knowledge autonomously and continuously, in domains unknown at design time and
with no predefined task boundaries. Its core is a network-memory long-term
memory coupled to a motivational engine of needs and drives from which goals
are discovered \cite{becerra2021,romero2022thesis}; knowledge lives in
\emph{cognitive nodes} ---perceptual classes (P-nodes), goals, world and utility
models, contexts (C-nodes) and policies \cite{romero2023ica,emdbnodes}--- which a
main cognitive loop activates from the current perception \cite{duro2019}. Its current ROS2
implementation \cite{emdbnodes} uses an \texttt{Episode} object
(\texttt{old\_perception}, policy, action, \texttt{perception},
\texttt{reward\_list}) as the unit of experience, and its \texttt{EpisodicBuffer}
---the short-term store from which world and utility models are trained--- is a
bounded \texttt{deque}: episodes are appended, the oldest discarded at the
limit, and read back by index or as a (optionally shuffled) batch. There is no
importance criterion, no similarity-based retrieval, and no consolidation. The
description of the buffer comes from that public code (file
\texttt{episodic\_buffer.py}), not from a publication. In the formulation of
Section \ref{sec:nas} its eviction and weighting correspond to the configuration
\texttt{\{read: nn, write: append, strength: constant, decay: 1.0, evict: fifo,
reinforce: 0.0\}}, one point among 576; because that point retrieves by nearest
neighbour, which the real buffer cannot, we call it the \emph{FIFO-NN proxy} of
the e-MDB buffer and treat it as an optimistic stand-in, not the deployed code.
Fig.~\ref{fig:integration} shows where EMBER would enter the architecture.

\section{Search over a bioinspired design space}
\label{sec:nas}

\subsection{The design space}

A \emph{configuration} in this work is a complete memory design: one choice on
each of six axes, where every axis is a decision an engineer building an episodic
memory has to make anyway, usually by intuition. How is a stored experience
retrieved ---by its single closest match, by a few, or by a whole neighborhood?
Is a repeated experience a new memory or a reinforcement of an existing one?
Does every experience encode with the same weight, or does surprise weight it?
Do unreinforced memories fade? When memory fills, what is discarded? And does
recalling a memory strengthen it? Table \ref{tab:axes} lists the six axes, their
options, and the biological mechanism each one stands for; Fig.\ \ref{fig:write}
shows how they compose into a single write. Enumerating the Cartesian product
---\result{exp03_axis_liveness:n_genotipos|int}{576} configurations--- means
every combination is tested, including the ones no designer would think to try.

Each configuration is realized by instantiating one parameterized engine with
its six values. The \result{exp03_axis_liveness:n_genotipos|int}{576} are
therefore not independently written systems but parameterizations of that single
engine, every mechanism implemented exactly once and shared by each
configuration that uses it: the same code path runs for every point, so any
score difference is attributable to the axis settings alone. Following the NAS
literature \cite{elsken2019}, we reserve \emph{architecture} for Section
\ref{sec:benchmark}, where it denotes four memory substrates differing in how a
trace is physically represented.

The \emph{read} axis operationalizes how past experiences are retrieved:
\texttt{nn} returns the single closest stored episode; \texttt{topk3}
aggregates the three closest; \texttt{radius} implements the full SDM read,
superposing all traces within a similarity radius relative to the best match.
The \emph{write} axis controls trace creation: \texttt{append} always creates a
new trace; \texttt{merge} first checks whether a sufficiently similar trace
already exists (cosine similarity $\geq 0.85$) and, if so, reinforces it
instead of creating a redundant copy ---the computational analogue of Hebbian
consolidation. The \emph{initial-strength} axis ---the \emph{strength gate},
which elsewhere in the text we also call the \emph{salience gate} when the
emphasis is on which biological signal opens it--- modulates how much weight a
new experience receives at encoding time: \texttt{constant} assigns a fixed
weight; the other three options introduce a biological signal ---novelty,
prediction error, or the two combined. The \emph{decay} axis determines whether
the strength of stored traces erodes over time without reinforcement. The
\emph{eviction} axis controls what is forgotten when memory fills:
\texttt{fifo} discards the oldest (the e-MDB default), \texttt{min\_strength}
discards the weakest trace, \texttt{min\_utility} discards the least-queried,
and \texttt{random} discards at random. The \emph{read-reinforcement} axis
models LTP by reactivation: each time an episode is retrieved, its strength
increases.

\subsection{Experimental protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

Every detail below is taken from the code that produced the results. Keys live in $\mathbb{R}^{32}$ and are normalized to
unit length at the memory boundary; every random draw derives from an explicit
seed, and within a seed all configurations receive the identical stream.

\emph{Stream generator.} A synthetic stream with $K_{proto}$ prototypes draws
$K_{proto}$ centres as i.i.d.\ Gaussian vectors normalized to the unit sphere
(nearly orthogonal at this dimension). Each routine experience picks a prototype
uniformly at random, adds isotropic Gaussian noise ($\sigma=0.05$ per
dimension) to its centre and renormalizes; its payload is the prototype
identity. Rare experiences have fresh independent random keys and unique
payloads, and are inserted at uniformly random positions. The generator also
supports correlated centres, power-law visit frequencies and drift; all three
are off in every reported experiment. CIFAR-100 streams (Section \ref{sec:real})
replace centre plus noise by a different image of one of $K_{proto}$ classes per
visit, draw rare experiences from disjoint classes, and keep the same
prediction-error ranges.
% TODO(exp10): familias de streams held-out (frecuencias desiguales, deriva,
% similitud ruidosa, prevalencia de raros) y resultados de robustez.

\emph{Surprise signals and importance labels.} Each experience carries a
prediction error drawn from a clipped normal: $\mathcal{N}(0.10,0.05)$ clipped
to $[0,0.35]$ for routine experiences and $\mathcal{N}(0.90,0.05)$ clipped to
$[0.65,1]$ for rare ones, so the two ranges do not overlap by construction.
Novelty is computed by the memory at write time as one minus the highest
cosine similarity to a stored trace (1 on an empty memory). The strength gate
assigns $s=1$ (\texttt{constant}), $1+2\nu$ (\texttt{novelty}), $1+2e$
(\texttt{pred\_error}) or $1+2\nu+2e$ (\texttt{both}). The importance label is
the generator's rare flag: it is used only to score and is never passed to the
memory, which sees key, payload and prediction error.

\emph{Phase 1, reconstruction gate} (capacity never binding). R1: 10 patterns,
cues keeping 30/50/70\,\% of the dimensions (the rest zeroed), scored by the
cosine similarity of the returned trace when its identity is correct and 0
otherwise. R2: 15 patterns under additive Gaussian noise
$\sigma\in\{0.1,0.2,0.3,0.4\}$, scored by identity accuracy. R3: capacity 2,
write $A$ then $B$, read $A$; 10 pairs, scored as R1. R4: accuracy at noise
$\sigma=0.15$ for loads $\{2,5,10,15,20,30\}$ with capacity equal to load,
averaged. The gate is the mean of R1--R4, with admission at $\geq 0.50$.

\emph{Phase 2, battery} ($C=20$). T1, rare-event retention: a stream with
$K_{proto}=5$, 300 routine and 20 rare experiences; every 5 writes the memory is
queried with a uniformly chosen, already written key (so read reinforcement can
act); at the end each rare experience is queried with its exact key, and counts
as retained if its payload is returned at similarity $\geq 0.85$. T2, noisy
retrieval under pressure: 60 independent random keys with prediction error
$\sim\mathcal{U}(0.05,0.95)$ are written into 20 slots; 120 queries pick an item
with probability proportional to its prediction error and add noise
$\sigma=0.30$; the score is the fraction returning the right payload. T3,
sequential interference: 4 blocks of 8 keys, each key written 3 times within
its block, prediction error 0.9 in the first block and 0.1 afterwards; the
score is the fraction of first-block keys retained as in T1. The
\emph{aggregate score} of a configuration is the unweighted mean of T1, T2 and
T3, each averaged over seeds.

\emph{Seeds and effect sizes.} The search and the axis audit use 3 seeds per
configuration; the threshold grid, 3 capacities $\times$ 8 ratios $\times$ 5
seeds on T1 alone, with 20 visits per prototype and $\max(5, C/2)$ rare
experiences; the substrate benchmark, 4 seeds; CIFAR-100, 5 ratios $\times$ 3
seeds at $C=20$. For an axis, $\eta^2 = SS_{between}/SS_{total}$ over the 576
configuration scores (the seed-averaged aggregate score in the search; T1 per
seed in the grid; seed-averaged T1 on CIFAR-100): a one-way, marginal decomposition that
averages over every other axis. The design is a full balanced factorial, so main
effects are orthogonal; two-way interactions are computed separately as
cell-mean residuals ($SS_{ab}/SS_{total}$) and are not folded into any axis's
$\eta^2$. Scores are deterministic given the seed, so there is no residual error
term and no significance test: $\eta^2$ is descriptive. In the grid, $\eta^2$ is
computed per seed and the reported band is a 95\,\% percentile bootstrap
(2000 resamples) over the five seed-level values ---the seed is the bootstrap
unit. The crossover of one capacity is the geometric mean of the last
write-dominant and first eviction-dominant $r$; the interval quoted for the mean
crossover bootstraps the three capacities' crossovers and is only descriptive.

\emph{Structural dependence between axes.} The axes are not functionally
independent, and the factorial decomposition does not assume they are. Trace
strength is consumed only by \texttt{min\_strength} eviction, so the
\texttt{strength} and \texttt{reinforce} axes are inert in the three quarters of
the space whose eviction never reads strength; \texttt{merge} acts only when the
merge threshold is reached; and under a constant gate without reinforcement
\texttt{min\_strength} reduces exactly to \texttt{fifo} (Section
\ref{sec:interaction}). A main effect therefore averages a mechanism over
configurations where it cannot act, and a small $\eta^2$ is not evidence that
the mechanism is weak. We read main effects only as attribution over the whole
space, and wherever a dependence is known we also report the conditional effect
within the configurations that consume the mechanism's output. The two-way
shares locate the dependence: \texttt{strength}$\times$\texttt{evict} explains
\result{exp01_nas_full:variance_share.strength×evict|pct}{4.3}\,\% of the
variance, three times the main effect of \texttt{strength}, and
\texttt{write}$\times$\texttt{evict}
\result{exp01_nas_full:variance_share.write×evict|pct}{7.8}\,\%.


\subsection{Axis observability}
\label{sec:axes}

An axis that changes the outcome nowhere is measuring nothing: any conclusion
about that mechanism's irrelevance would be a conclusion about the instrument.
Before reporting effects we verify all six axes are observable, by measuring the
maximum performance difference between configurations differing only in that
axis. All six clear this check, and an axis-liveness audit runs in continuous
integration
so that none can lapse unnoticed as the space evolves.

Write mode is the strongest main effect
(\result{exp01_nas_full:main_effects.0.eta2|pct}{56.6}\,\%), followed by the
eviction policy (\result{exp01_nas_full:main_effects.1.eta2|pct}{17.0}\,\%).
Decay and initial strength explain much less as main effects
(\result{exp01_nas_full:main_effects.2.eta2|pct}{1.8}\,\% and
\result{exp01_nas_full:main_effects.3.eta2|pct}{1.4}\,\% respectively), and read
and read-reinforcement come out near zero as main effects
(\result{exp01_nas_full:main_effects.5.eta2|pct}{0.0}\,\% and
\result{exp01_nas_full:main_effects.4.eta2|pct}{0.0}\,\%), though both have
strictly positive observability
(\result{exp01_nas_full:main_effects.5.liveness|f3}{0.119} and
\result{exp01_nas_full:main_effects.4.liveness|f3}{0.206}): a null $\eta^2$ with
positive observability does not say the mechanism does not matter, only that its
effect is not a main effect ---Section \ref{sec:interaction} shows exactly that
case for initial strength.

The FIFO-NN proxy of the e-MDB buffer ---configuration
\texttt{\{read: nn, write: append, strength: constant, decay: 1.0, evict: fifo,
reinforce: 0.0\}}--- scores
\result{exp01_nas_full:incumbent.score|f3}{0.106}, in the range
\result{exp01_nas_full:incumbent.rank_optimistic|int}{416}--%
\result{exp01_nas_full:incumbent.rank_pessimistic|int}{469} of 576, with
\result{exp01_nas_full:incumbent.n_tied|int}{54} ties. It is not the absolute
floor of the space: below its pessimistic rank of
\result{exp01_nas_full:incumbent.rank_pessimistic|int}{469}\ there are
configurations that score strictly worse, almost all combining criterion-free
\texttt{append} writing with minimum-utility eviction or \texttt{topk3} reads
over compressed memory. The frontier of the space scores
\result{exp01_nas_full:frontier.score|f3}{0.818}: a factor of almost eight over
the incumbent.

\subsection{The regime transition}

The \result{exp01_nas_full:main_effects.0.eta2|pct}{56.6}\,\% of the write axis is not primarily a property of the merge
mechanism; it is a property of the relationship between the number of recurring
experience prototypes and memory capacity ---the ratio $r$. We sweep the
configuration space over a grid of capacities and ratios, holding visits per
prototype fixed so that $r$ is not confounded with stream recurrence.

The transition from ``the write axis explains most of the variance'' to ``the
eviction axis does'' falls abruptly near $r=1$ across the three evaluated
capacities (10, 20 and 40),
independently of $K_{proto}$: the estimated crossover (geometric mean over the
transition cell, averaged over capacities) is
\result{exp02_threshold_grid:r_umbral_medio|f2}{0.88}, with a descriptive
bootstrap interval over the three capacities of
[\result{exp02_threshold_grid:r_umbral_ci.0|f2}{0.87},
\result{exp02_threshold_grid:r_umbral_ci.1|f2}{0.89}]. The mechanism is simpler than the statistics that localize it. Eviction never
runs while the memory has room: a trace is discarded only when a write would
exceed capacity. Below $r=1$ the recurring experience \emph{fits}, merging
compresses it into fewer slots, and the freed slots become available for the
rare, so what matters is how one writes. Above $r=1$ there is nothing to
compress, memory is saturated, eviction runs on nearly every write, and the
question reduces to which policy best preserves what matters. We call
these the \emph{compression} and \emph{selection} regimes, and the hypothesis of
this section is that which of the two a memory is operating in, rather than any
property of the mechanisms themselves, determines which mechanism matters.

That mechanism also shows why the transition is expected where it falls: it is
close to a restatement of what capacity means. When the prototypes fit,
consolidation can hold them without displacing anything; when they do not, some
form of selection is unavoidable. What the grid adds is that the switch in
attribution is abrupt and lands at the same $r$ for three capacities, not that a
switch exists. It is established for one stationary generator with equal
prototype frequencies, fixed recurrence, isotropic noise and a fixed share of rare
events; whether $r$ is the governing variable or
one component of a broader capacity-pressure relationship under unequal
frequencies, drift, noisier similarity, other rare-event prevalences,
stream lengths and a wider capacity range is untested here, and we
state it as a hypothesis supported under the evaluated conditions.

\subsection{The interaction between salience and eviction}
\label{sec:interaction}

The \result{exp01_nas_full:main_effects.3.eta2|pct}{1.4}\,\% main effect of
initial strength is misleading read on its own.
Strength modulation influences behavior only when a mechanism \emph{reads} that
signal, and of the four eviction policies only \texttt{min\_strength} does.
Conditioning on \texttt{\{evict: min\_strength, write: append\}}, a constant
gate leaves the policy nothing to rank by
(\result{exp01_nas_full:salience_conditional.constant|pct}{3.6}\,\%), whereas
novelty and prediction error together make it selective
(\result{exp01_nas_full:salience_conditional.both|pct}{52.0}\,\%): a conditional
improvement of
\result{exp01_nas_full:salience_conditional_ratio|f2}{14.41}$\times$, entirely
attributable to this interaction. The factor should be read for what it is. On
this benchmark the prediction errors of routine and rare experiences come from
non-overlapping ranges (Section \ref{sec:protocol}), so once an eviction rule
reads strength, keeping the rare events follows almost directly from the
construction: the number shows that the salience signal has no effect until
something consumes it, not that selecting what matters from a realistic signal
is solved. How much of the gain survives overlapping, noisy or misleading
signals is a separate question.

configurations. The frontier differs from the incumbent proxy on exactly two of
the six axes: the strength gate (\texttt{constant} $\to$ \texttt{both}) and the

\subsection{The frontier architecture}
\label{sec:frontier}

The best-scoring point of the space is
\texttt{\{read: nn, write: append, strength: both, decay: 1.0, evict:
min\_strength, reinforce: 0.0\}}, at
\result{exp01_nas_full:frontier.score|f3}{0.818} against
\result{exp01_nas_full:incumbent.score|f3}{0.106}. In practice: every experience
is stored as its own trace, written with a weight proportional to how surprising
it was. Weights never decay and are never revised on recall, so importance is
fixed at encoding. Recall returns the closest trace, and when memory fills the
lowest-weight trace is discarded --- by construction the least surprising thing
held.

Two properties matter more than the rank. It \emph{appends}: the axis that
dominates the variance below $r=1$ is the write mode, but the winning setting
never consolidates. Merging compresses routine into fewer slots, yet accumulates
strength geometrically on whatever recurs, so a prototype visited thirty times
outweighs a rare event encoded once under maximum surprise. And it is
\emph{regime-neutral}: never depending on merging, it does not care whether $r$
is above or below the threshold, and always consulting the importance signal it
behaves identically in both. That is what makes it a deployable recommendation
rather than the winner of one cell --- a robot running this configuration need
not know, or estimate, its own $r$.

\section{Comparative architecture evaluation}
\label{sec:benchmark}

The search of Section \ref{sec:nas} ranks configurations of one engine on
retention under capacity pressure, and the winning one reads by nearest
neighbour ---a mode that never exercises a distributed representation. It leaves
a prior question untested: whether each memory \emph{substrate}, the physical
scheme by which a trace is stored, can perform the operation any associative
memory must support ---reconstructing a complete pattern from a noisy or partial
cue. This section runs a two-phase benchmark that first admits substrates by
reconstruction fidelity (R1--R4, no capacity pressure) and then re-tests them
under continuous pressure (T1--T3). The four are SDM, ENN (pure Hebbian memory),
Spiking-SDM (SDM with LIF-neuron activation) and a pure spiking circuit
(LIF + STDP), with the FIFO-NN proxy (``FIFO'' in Table \ref{tab:benchmark})
again included as a point in the same space.
Table \ref{tab:benchmark} summarizes both phases: R1 recovers a masked pattern,
R2 a noisy one, R3 checks whether $B$ overwrites $A$, R4 traces accuracy against
load; T1 is rare-event retention, T2 noisy retrieval under pressure, T3 how much
of an early block survives later ones.

The pure spiking circuit fails pattern reconstruction and noise robustness

result is not a flaw of the spiking mechanism itself ---it is the direct
motivation for EMBER's PoC-5: an integrated core combining SDM (for the
continuous-to-distributed mapping) with ENN (for the trace lifecycle).

items into a memory of capacity twenty, with the prediction error of common and
rare experiences drawn from non-overlapping ranges, so an architecture that

the exact-fit ceiling but not the separability: its prediction-error ranges are
the same, so it measures which axis carries the variance, not how hard
selection is.

\section{Validation on real perceptual embeddings}
\label{sec:real}

The preceding experiments run on random Gaussian vectors in $\mathbb{R}^{32}$,
nearly orthogonal at that dimension. This reduces cross-interference and could
overestimate retrieval accuracy relative to real embeddings, which have
correlated structure and uneven cluster density. We therefore instrument the
same grid on streams built from CIFAR-100 \cite{krizhevsky2009} embeddings
extracted with a pretrained ResNet-18 \cite{he2016} and reduced by principal
component analysis (PCA) to the working dimension.

The result is not that the threshold shifts: it is that, with this extractor, the write axis is inoperative in all
five cells of the sweep, explaining between
\result{exp05_real_embeddings:dominios.cifar100.celdas.4.eta2_write|pct}{0.0}\,\%
and
\result{exp05_real_embeddings:dominios.cifar100.celdas.1.eta2_write|pct}{0.4}\,\%
of the variance regardless of the ratio. The deciding quantity is \emph{intra-prototype similarity}: how alike two
encounters with the same kind of thing look to the memory, as a cosine.
Consolidation is possible only when it clears the merge threshold, since merging
is triggered by exactly that comparison --- if two photographs of one class do
not look alike enough, the memory cannot recognize them as the same situation
and stores both. The measured median is
\result{exp05_real_embeddings:precondicion_cifar100.similitud_intra_prototipo|f3}{0.401},
well below the merge threshold
(\result{exp05_real_embeddings:precondicion_cifar100.umbral_de_fusion|f2}{0.85}),
so merging almost never triggers and $K_{effective}$ ---the traces the routine
occupies after consolidation--- grows without bound instead of converging to
$K_{proto}$: from
\result{exp05_real_embeddings:dominios.cifar100.celdas.0.k_efectivo|int}{84}\
to \result{exp05_real_embeddings:dominios.cifar100.celdas.4.k_efectivo|int}{1435}\
traces as nominal $K$ rises from 5 to 80, with capacity fixed at 20. The
synthetic domain in the same run crosses normally
($r \approx \result{exp05_real_embeddings:dominios.synthetic.r_umbral|f2}{0.77}$),
so the null result is specific to this extractor, not a failure of the grid.
Lowering the threshold does not rescue consolidation, because intra-class
similarity (median
\result{exp07_merge_sensitivity:umbral_fusion.cifar100.similitudes.intra.p50|f2}{0.38})
overlaps inter-class similarity (95th percentile
\result{exp07_merge_sensitivity:umbral_fusion.cifar100.similitudes.inter.p95|f2}{0.42}):
no threshold from 0.30 to 0.97 yields merges both frequent and pure, and
from 0.40 up eviction dominates at every $r$. At 0.30 the write axis
regains dominance at low $r$ only because merging destroys: just
\result{exp07_merge_sensitivity:umbral_fusion.cifar100.umbrales.t030.pureza_fusion_r_min|pct}{47.7}\,\%
of merges join the same class,
\result{exp07_merge_sensitivity:umbral_fusion.cifar100.umbrales.t030.raros_absorbidos_r_min|pct}{70.0}\,\%
of rare events are absorbed on arrival, and the best merging configuration
retains
\result{exp07_merge_sensitivity:umbral_fusion.cifar100.umbrales.t030.celdas.r0p25.puntaje_mejor_merge.media|f2}{0.30}
against
\result{exp07_merge_sensitivity:umbral_fusion.cifar100.umbrales.t030.celdas.r0p25.puntaje_frontera.media|f2}{1.00}
for the \texttt{append} frontier. The precondition is therefore separability
---a threshold that repeated encounters clear and distinct ones do not--- not
the calibration of 0.85.

Intra-prototype similarity is not a term in $r$; what it decides is whether $r$
is well defined at all. Where consolidation is reachable but only partial, it
acts on the ratio through $K_{effective}$: sub-threshold visits fragment a
prototype into several traces, so the effective ratio exceeds the nominal one
and the hypothesis holds when stated over it. Where consolidation is unreachable
$K_{effective}$ has no stable value ---it grows with stream length instead of
converging--- so this precondition is not a special case of an effective ratio
but a separate binary check that comes first, and on CIFAR-100 it already fails:
no recomputation of $r$, nominal or effective, produces a compression regime
where there is none.

On a real stream $K_{proto}$ must be estimated. We cluster the keys over a sweep
of candidate counts and take the count maximizing the mean \emph{silhouette
coefficient}, which for each vector compares its distance to its own cluster
against its distance to the nearest other one: near $1$ means tight,
well-separated clusters, near $0$ means the partition is arbitrary. Clustering
returns groups whether or not any exist ---on isotropic noise the whole sweep
scores $0.04$--$0.07$ with no peak, yet still yields an argmax--- so we require a
silhouette of at least $0.15$ before treating $r$ as defined, and report a
bootstrap interval rather than a point integer.

On CIFAR-100 that estimation becomes uninformative: the intervals reach the
ceiling of the search in several cells, consistent with the absence of compact
cluster structure in this domain. This is a further reason to treat merge
reachability ---measured directly as a similarity comparison, with no clustering
involved--- as the robust diagnostic, rather than a derived $\hat K$.

\section{Validation in a real environment under partial observability}
\label{sec:minigrid}

CIFAR-100 tests the regime hypothesis on real data, but it is still a
hand-built stream from static embeddings. A robot does not receive a stream: it
receives the consequences of its own actions, with partial observation and
temporal structure that no generator reproduces. We instrument the
\texttt{ember.envs} adapter on \texttt{MiniGrid-MemoryS13-v0} \cite{minigrid},
a navigation environment designed specifically to demand memory: the agent must
remember an object seen at the start of the episode in order to choose
correctly at the end. We compare the frontier configuration from Section
\ref{sec:nas} against the FIFO with \texttt{t1\_rare\_retention} ---the same
task that measures rarity throughout the rest of the program--- across three
conditions that change one variable at a time (Table \ref{tab:minigrid}).

\textbf{Baseline.} With a uniform random policy, both the frontier and the FIFO
sit at the rare-event retention floor. The cause is not a retrieval error
---with no capacity pressure the frontier recovers any stored trace with

linear transition model over the observation, does not separate the rewarded

experiences have a prediction error equal to or greater than the least

Table \ref{tab:minigrid}: the smaller it is, the more cleanly the surprise
signal separates what mattered from what did not.

\textbf{Ruling out data scarcity.} The simplest explanation must be ruled out
first. A policy biased toward moving forward over turning ---which does not
touch the surprise computation, only what gets visited--- raises the
rewarded-episode count from \result{exp06_minigrid:comparacion_aleatoria_prediccion.n_raros_total|int}{38} to \result{exp06_minigrid:comparacion_sesgada_prediccion.n_raros_total|int}{156} on the same step budget. Retention stays at the floor,

nothing, so the problem is not a small sample.

\textbf{The signal, not the quantity.} The strength gate is motivated by the
dopaminergic account of prediction error, but in its canonical form that
account is about \emph{reward}-prediction error \cite{schultz1997}, and so is
the behavioural evidence that prediction error enhances episodic memory
\cite{rouhani2018} ---not perceptual prediction error. We replace the transition
model with a reward predictor ---same online linear architecture, same random

just as in the baseline. The two intervals do not overlap despite the small

events from the same rollout may be correlated; the rollout is the more

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{The regime hypothesis and its practical implication}

The ratio $r = K_{proto}/C$ has a direct operational reading. In an early,
structured environment ---a warehouse, a lab--- the robot sees the same things
repeatedly, $r < 1$, and consolidation is efficient; in a heterogeneous one $r$
grows above 1 and selective eviction by relevance dominates. The recommendation
is not to pick a regime: the frontier architecture works in both, never
depending on merging (which fails above $r=1$) and always exploiting the
importance signal.

\subsection{The spiking circuit and the motivation for PoC-5}

The spiking circuit's failure at the reconstruction gate is a property of the
domain, not the mechanism: PoC-3 formed reliable engrams with LIF+STDP in its
native discrete setting. What is missing is the bridge from continuous vectors
to discrete assemblies ---EMBER's PoC-5, pairing SDM for the
continuous-to-distributed mapping with ENN for the trace lifecycle, as a single
\emph{Cognitive Node}.

\subsection{The FIFO contrast, and the precondition as a diagnostic}

Mapping the e-MDB FIFO to a configuration is deliberately generous, so the
FIFO-NN proxy is an optimistic stand-in and not the deployed buffer: the real
\texttt{EpisodicBuffer} has no content-addressable retrieval at all, and returns
episodes by position or as training batches. We chose nearest-neighbour reads to
isolate eviction and weighting, the two axes on which the frontier differs.
Even so the proxy ranks
\result{exp01_nas_full:incumbent.rank_optimistic|int}{416}--%
\result{exp01_nas_full:incumbent.rank_pessimistic|int}{469} of 576 and is the
floor at retaining rare events among substrates, though not at reconstruction.

% sin búsqueda por contenido) como baseline adicional; reportar aquí su
% retención y reconstrucción frente al proxy FIFO-NN.
This is not a criticism of e-MDB, built for a different primary goal ---its
buffer feeds model training, not recall--- but a specific claim: as a store of
what to remember, the buffer as implemented is inadequate for the LOLA regime,
with a quantified gap for its most favourable proxy.

The finding of Section \ref{sec:real} gives the robot a check it can run online:
intra-prototype similarity, measured on the vectors the memory already receives
and compared against inter-prototype similarity, decides ---before the write axis
matters at all--- whether consolidation is worth attempting, with no external
instrumentation.

\subsection{The source of surprise matters as much as its existence}

Section \ref{sec:minigrid} generalizes this beyond merging. The synthetic and
CIFAR-100 designs share a silent assumption: that the surprise signal arrives
aligned with what the robot needs to remember. That is free when a generator
sets it by hand; in a real environment one must choose \emph{what to predict},
and that choice decides whether the mechanism has anything to protect. The
configuration does not change ---only the signal it is fed--- so the salience
gate
inherits the quality of its input, and choosing that input is a design decision
as consequential as any axis of the space.

\section{Limitations and future work}

The real-data validation of Section \ref{sec:real} uses one extractor
(ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18, PCA to 32 dimensions), one dataset and one
capacity; whether the intra-class similarity of
\result{exp05_real_embeddings:precondicion_cifar100.similitud_intra_prototipo|f3}{0.401}
is a property of the visual domain, the extractor, or both is untested; a
fine-tuned extractor could change it, a lower merge threshold does not. The
MiniGrid validation likewise uses one environment and one exploration policy
besides the forward-biased one.

Three further limits are not addressed by any experiment in this revision.
EMBER has not been integrated into the running e-MDB: Fig.~\ref{fig:integration}
is a design, the incumbent is a proxy rather than the deployed buffer, and
whether better retention improves the world and utility models e-MDB trains from
its buffer is untested. All real-data evidence comes from a single visual
extractor. And no experiment ran on a physical robot.

The most consequential open question is a tension between our strongest result
and the setting that motivates the work. Section \ref{sec:minigrid} resolves the
salience precondition by anchoring surprise in reward ---but LOLA is defined by
the \emph{absence} of predefined goals, and an open-ended robot may face long
stretches with no reward to predict. The available signal then falls back to
perceptual novelty, which is precisely where we measured retention at the floor.
The fix is valid where a reward exists and unproven where it does not, which is
the regime e-MDB is ultimately built for. Intrinsic motivation, learning
progress, or the needs and drives of e-MDB's motivational engine
\cite{becerra2021} are testable substitutes
within the same protocol; none is evaluated here. How to define salience when
importance is delayed, ambiguous or unrelated to any external reward remains
open, and it is the question on which the usefulness of the salience gate for
LOLA ultimately rests. Finally, because no result was obtained on a physical
robot, real sensor noise, continuous control and lifelong non-stationarity
remain untested, and the generator's distributional-drift parameter is never
activated in the reported experiments.

\section{Conclusion}

We present EMBER, an investigation of bioinspired episodic memory for cognitive
robots. On the synthetic streams evaluated, exhaustive architecture search
indicates that which memory mechanism matters is not fixed but set by the
operating regime $r = K_{proto}/C$: below a threshold near 1 the write axis
governs retention, above it eviction does ---a hypothesis tied to how capacity
is defined, whose robustness beyond this generator remains to be shown. The
encoding-salience mechanism the literature most motivates has a negligible main
effect, yet conditioned on the one eviction policy that reads its signal it
recovers fourteen times the rare-event retention ---not weak, but gated. The
benchmark adds that SDM with a prediction-error gate is the only substrate
pairing reconstruction fidelity with perfect retention on a stream whose rare
events are separable by construction, that the FIFO-NN proxy of the e-MDB buffer
reconstructs well but is the floor at retention ---its deficiency is choosing,
not remembering--- and that the pure spiking circuit does not transfer to
continuous vectors, marking PoC-5 as the next milestone. Real data adds two
preconditions: consolidation needs a merge threshold that repeated encounters
clear and distinct ones do not, which no threshold achieves on CIFAR-100, turning a condition of the regime
hypothesis into an online-measurable check; and salience needs surprise anchored in reward,
under which retention rises from the floor to more than half with no change of
configuration or policy. What the salience mechanism needs is the right signal.
